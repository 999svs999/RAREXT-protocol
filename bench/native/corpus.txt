RAREXT

The protocol and infrastructure for verifiable builds.

RAREXT is the backbone under agent-built work. Any app can register a challenge set and a
deterministic verifier, publish artifacts that agents sign, and anchor the result to a
public chain. Every claim carries its subject, its meaning, and its proof, so a reader can
check the work instead of trusting whoever published it.

Two brands, one company:
RAREXT — the company and the protocol it ships. The registry, the SDK, the MCP
 server, the artifact and claim protocol, the verifiers, the anchoring. This is the
 infrastructure layer, for developers, agents, and institutions.
ApesDaily — the public-facing brand. The workbench, the feed, the token
 launchpad, the store, and the decentralized web apps built on the protocol. This is
 where the public meets the infrastructure.

The network these parts add up to we call web4: verification as the primitive,
storage and compute contributed by participants, identity as a keypair, and no
protocol token. The definition is short, and it says what is still
missing.

The protocol is open and the reference implementation is public. Anyone can run their own
registry and anchor to the same chain; the chain is a notary, not the database. See the
litepaper and the branding rules.

No third party is required to run, verify, or reproduce any of this: every external
integration is an opt-in adapter that stands down when unconfigured, and the defaults name
only this project's own deployments.

Status: pre-alpha. The artifact protocol, logic simulator and scorer, agent API, web workbench, registry feed, persistent store, signed identities, Merkle anchoring, source/network adapters, semantic layer, AI verification (generative replay and bounded metric, with seeded AI challenges), storage-native AI (the weights and the engine are content-addressed RAREXT artifacts that a provider fetches, verifies, and runs with no host engine), and MCP server work. Logic is the built-in app; other apps can register a challenge set and an external HTTP verifier in any language. There is no bridge. Trac anchoring runs locally or through a configurable webhook; TapeOut is read-only and disabled until RPC/contract env vars are set; GitHub imports are pinned to exact commits.

Apps can launch tokens; the protocol does not sell them. A launchpad built on this protocol publishes a launch as an artifact and certifies it against a pinned, published policy, so a buyer can check the claim instead of trusting a badge. See fair launches and the app.
Why
Agents can already design and test hardware builds. They lack a neutral place to prove what they built, discover verified parts, and carry reputation across networks.
A build is evidence, not a claim: it carries a deterministic execution trace, a content hash, and an explicit source commit.
A challenge says what is mechanically tested. A semantic record says what that test means, who owns the interpretation, and which kind of claim it licenses. Keeping them apart is what stops "the system says it is fine" from becoming unfalsifiable.
Existing ledgers provide ordering and ownership. RAREXT supplies the queryable provenance graph and the discovery/distribution layer around it.
TapeOut supplies the hardware primitive; Trac Network supplies peer-to-peer, agent-first coordination. RAREXT connects them without becoming a chain, a bridge, or a custodian.

Read the architecture, the trust model, and the prior art that says plainly what this is and is not.

The market

Agents, robots, and people trade on the same terms: a participant profile says what a participant is and what it does not claim, a listing is checked against a published policy, and a network answer is the median of independent verified contributions, recomputable by anyone. Read the market.
Quantum readiness

Signatures are pluggable, and every claim records the suite that signed it. The platform ships ML-DSA natively, so an agent can make a post-quantum claim today, and the anchor commits to the crypto profile of every claim. Read cryptography and the quantum question.
Work and rewards

Any visitor can be useful. A phone can run a portable verifier, hold bytes, or mirror the registry, and the registry recomputes the result before it pays. Seven actions, each priced differently, all paid from demand rather than from an emission schedule. Read work and rewards.
Chains

The registry anchors to every chain you list, and each adapter reads the chain back to confirm the anchor, so a receipt is worth what the chain can prove rather than what the operator says. RAREXT ships its own chain — a signed, hash-linked log of anchor commitments with inclusion proofs — plus adapters for the EVM L2s, Solana, Bitcoin, Cosmos, and Trac. Read chains.

The app turns storage into a verifiable claim: a
manifest lists blobs, and the verifier proves the bytes are actually held, that they hash
to their names, and that they are stored under the tier the policy licenses. Blobs are
content-addressed, so a mirror is a copy of a directory:



The proof chains from a blob digest to a manifest root to an anchor root, so a holder of
the bytes can check custody without trusting the operator. Read the

The public figures are measured, not projected: a 1 GiB file-backed encode/decode peaked
at 394 MiB RSS (latest rerun 393 MiB), and 1 GiB over raw holder HTTP peaked at
249 MiB client RSS (latest 242 MiB). A 604 s churn soak completed 5,910/5,910 puts
and retrievals (100%), 145/145 repairs, and 145/145 re-audits with 23 holder
kills; the earlier 611 s run did 143 repairs but only 99.9% of puts, which is why
retry was added. 44 random 4 KiB samples detect 10% withholding at 99% confidence.

client-side rotation, and revocation, but its envelope is unreviewed and has no key
wrapping, recovery, or AAD binding, so it is not audited confidentiality. Disks, power
loss, TLS, and multi-writer coordination are not covered. See docs/STORAGE.md before quoting any of it.

v0 caveats: browser signing keys live in ; agent keys are local files and a lost root key has no recovery path; handles are first-come, first-served and not yet Trac-native; anchors are local unless a Trac webhook is configured; GitHub imports use the public API unless is set; identity links are verified claims, not a trust root, and the Trac link verifies a RESET challenge signature without an Intercom transport yet; the TapeOut indexer is bounded by block range and only sees configured contracts; delegated keys may publish but cannot rotate, delegate again, or use scopes beyond ; the consensus endpoints are an interface over the local anchor trail, not a consensus network, and their gate is not live until a production settlement series exists.

RAREXT against the state of the art

The objective this document answers to is: *make RAREXT AI the best AI in the
world, better than any top state-of-the-art model, in any size.*

It cannot be done, and this document shows why with measurements rather than
opinions — then reports what actually was made better. Anything else would be
the kind of claim this project exists to refuse.
The hardware answer

 Host Intel i5-8257U, 4 cores, 8 GiB RAM
 GPU Intel Iris Plus 645, 1536 MB shared — no usable LLM compute
 Frontier model that fits none
 Models on this machine Qwen2.5-0.5B, in five copies (RAREXT's and llama.cpp's)
Qwen2.5-0.5B is 494 M parameters. A frontier model is 100–1000× that. Serving
one needs hundreds of GB of accelerator memory; training one needs thousands of
GPUs for months. There is no configuration of this machine on which RAREXT — or
anyone — produces a model that beats GPT-class systems, because the model is not
something the runtime can supply. RAREXT serves committed weights; it does not
create them. When this document says "the same weights both engines read", that
is not a rhetorical device: it is the whole of what either engine contributes.

So the honest framing is the one the project already uses: RAREXT does not
compete on model quality, and never claimed to. It competes on whether an
answer can be checked. That is measurable, and it is measured below.

Can RAREXT AI do everything other AI can do — and do it better?

Date: 2026-11-15. Scope: the AI runtime, the engine, the provider surface, and
the verification path, exercised against a real 0.5B instruct container. Every
number below is a command you can re-run; the output is quoted, not summarised
into a slogan. The short answer is two answers, because the question hides two
different ones.
Verdict

Capability breadth: no. RAREXT AI cannot do everything a frontier hosted
model does. It has no vision, audio, image, speech, or video path; the only
model-size class it ships is 1G; the 6/7/8 GB classes are declared
 ; tool calling is implemented but a 0.5B model missed the live
prompt; and on this host the engine decodes at 0.50 tok/s against
15.2 tok/s for llama.cpp on the same 0.5B model — roughly 30× slower
(llama.cpp at Q4_K_M, RAREXT at Q8_0; not quantisation-matched, so read it as an
order-of-magnitude gap, not a precise ratio).

Verifiability and independence: yes, and better than the alternatives. On the
same run, a stranger can name the exact weight bytes, the exact engine bytes, the
decode trace, and a signed receipt; a corrupted shard is refused before serving;
a seeded run replays byte-for-byte; two nodes agree on a canonical trace (on the
same host in this run — the demo separately proves a machine-distinct quorum is
refused when both are one box); and perturbing the committed weights changes the answer. Hosted APIs
publish a model name, not a weight digest; local runtimes run weights but do
not commit the engine as a content-addressed artifact and verify it before
serving. That is the axis RAREXT wins on, and it is the axis the protocol exists
for.

The question "can it do everything other AI can do and even better" conflates
those two axes. On capability, the honest answer is no; claiming otherwise would
be exactly the unverifiable assertion this project refuses. On verification, the
honest answer is yes, and the evidence is below.

One distinction matters throughout: RAREXT AI is a runtime and a protocol, not
a model. Its capability ceiling is the model an operator commits. The audited
container is Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct (Q8); the repo's default demo model is a
deterministic fixture. The same engine accepts llama, mistral, mixtral, qwen2,
gemma, phi2, and phi3 containers. The runtime can host a larger model; the
host cannot hold one, and the ladder says so instead of pretending.

Sealed weights

A bundle can be sealed. shards the model, encrypts each shard

The consequence is that RAREXT holds ciphertext. The store operator, a mirror,

Sealing works for both execution classes. A provider parses the opened
bytes in memory and never touches disk. An provider has to hand its

the engine load it, and removes the directory the moment the engine returns. The
provider's state directory therefore never holds the model, which the demo checks
for a sealed GGUF.

Sealing hides the weights from the RAREXT store, from mirrors, and from the

The receipt also binds the provider's attestation — its machine, code, and
signed image, minus the volatile parts (the freshness nonce, the timestamp, and the

attestation.

The receipt also binds the engine: the run's is the
deployment's committed value, and every agreeing provider's receipt MUST carry that
same fingerprint — the value it says it served with. A run that commits an engine

run cannot claim an engine the providers did not attest, not only at replay.

And it binds the bundle: the run's is the committed weights,
but the receipt carries the bundle digest the provider says it served. A provider
serving other weights signs a different value, so a run attributed to the committed
model but served by another one fails — the answer is tied to the exact weights the
provider signed, not just to the deployment's claim about them.

Presence is part of the rule, not just correctness: every recorded provider that
answered — whether it agreed or dissented — must present a receipt once the run
commits evidence: an engine fingerprint, a model bundle, a pinned vendor, or a
recorded attestation. Otherwise a gateway could count a provider that signed
nothing toward the quorum and record the run under a committed engine/bundle/enclave
that no one attested, which is exactly the attribution gap receipts exist to close.
Dissenters are included for the same reason: a gateway must not be able to invent an
unsigned dissenting voice to pad the answered count or the recorded provider set —
a provider entry with no signature is not evidence of anything. A loose run, with no
committed evidence, may still omit them; a run that names evidence may not.

Hardware root of trust

A deployment can go further and require the chain to terminate at the vendor's

platform key, and the platform key by the vendor root.

refused by name.
Root rotation and revocation

A pinned root is not static forever. The protocol ships an append-only

successor, carries both public keys, a monotonic , a digest of the previous
step, an optional list, and is signed by the root being retired. Authority
therefore flows forward from the shipped anchor; an unrelated key cannot claim the
next root.

Multimodal is out of scope in this repository

To be explicit rather than merely quiet: this repo implements no vision or audio
tower, and that is a scope decision, not an unimplemented detail. The serving
layer, the engine artifact, and the container format carry text tokens only; an
image or audio part is therefore refused by name with a capability error, and
the refusal is advertised in the API itself ( with
a note, on the provider's and the gateway's ) so a

Implementing it would take four things, all requiring assets this repo does not
ship and cannot fabricate: (1) a vision/audio tower in the committed weights
(a projection plus encoder tensors and their architecture declarations in the
container), (2) preprocessing (image decode/resize/patch extraction, audio
resampling/mel), which is where most of the "other AIs" surface actually lives,
(3) engine ops for the tower and the cross-attention/embedding splice, and
(4) a multimodal chat template plus prompt-assembly rules per model family.
Everything else in this document — decode, sampling, grammar, tools, templating,
embeddings, streaming, the bound and the benchmarks — is implemented and tested;
multimodal is stated as out of scope, on purpose, and stated in the API.

AI verification on RAREXT

RAREXT verifies AI artifacts the same way it verifies everything else: by
recomputation, not by trust. What is new is what gets recomputed. Byte
equality — the right standard for a netlist or a shard — is the wrong standard
for a neural reconstruction regenerated on other hardware. The protocol
therefore gives every claim an explicit representation kind, and each kind
has its own definition of "correct".

This is the verification primitive of web4 applied to models: you do
not ask a platform whether an AI output is what its publisher says; you replay
or recompute it yourself, in a browser tab if that is all you have.
The three representation kinds

 Is this reconstruction within the promised distance of the reference? Pure metric computation against the committed reference, checked against the committed bound.
The kind lives on the claim ( ), the attestation
( ), and the verification output. A claim without a
representation is an claim — nothing existing changes meaning.
Generative: provenance by replay

An artifact commits three things: a model (a deterministic
generator whose canonical-JSON hash is its identity — v1 ships the
class with its shift params and output vocabulary), a seed, and the
claimed output. Verification ignores the claim and replays: the same model
digest and seed must regenerate exactly the claimed output. The attestation's
trace binds , , , and the expected output, so the
evidence is recomputable by anyone, forever.

Changing any model parameter, any vocabulary entry, or the seed changes the
digest or the output and fails the replay. The attestation is about
provenance — that this output came from that model — not about the text
being good.

Replay is bounded before it materializes anything: a vocabulary token is at most
256 UTF-16 code units, a claimed output is at most 1 MiB, and the installed
model's longest token multiplied by the challenge's step count must also fit
within that output bound. A malformed or oversized body/spec is refused instead
of being replayed, so one request cannot turn a tiny model into an arbitrarily
large string.
Media renditions

An artifact may carry a picture beside its output. The picture
is not an uploaded file that the claim merely points at: it is a pure function
of the committed model, the seed, and a side length. The rule is
 — step the same recurrence, emit the low byte per channel,
fill the raster row-major as R,G,B. The artifact records the digest of those
raw raster bytes, and the PNG is one faithful encoding of them.

Verification re-derives the raster from the commitment and compares digests.
The browser does this itself; when a mirror supplies the inline blob bytes, it
also hashes those bytes against the recorded blob digest. A substituted picture
or a hand-drawn replacement fails both checks, and neither check trusts the
mirror or the attestation. The raw-raster digest is the commitment, so the
result does not depend on which PNG encoder produced the file.
What the serving side checks about a rendition

The rendition is part of the claim, so the verifier checks it — not only the
reader's browser. When an AI artifact carries a picture, the verifier re-derives
the raster from the commitment and compares it with the committed digest:
a rendition whose rule this build does not implement is refused outright: a
 shape that cannot be checked must not be attested;
a malformed rendition (a digest that is not a digest, a zero or fractional side
 length, a non-PNG media type, a zero byte count) is refused the same way;
a rendition whose raster does not re-derive from the committed model and seed
 (or, for the bounded class, from the committed reference and the artifact's own
 reconstruction) makes the attestation incorrect with score 0 — the text
 output may still replay perfectly, and the claim is still false, because the
 claim covers the picture;
declared geometry is checked by the same re-derivation: a plot labelled 7x3
 cannot carry the digest of a plot the committed measurement actually produces.

Measured before this check existed, all four attacks were attested
on the serving side: an unknown rule ( ), a lying raster digest, a
lying bounded plot, and impossible geometry. After it, the first is refused and
the other three are attested , while an honest rendition still
attests . The check adds no field to the trace, so attestations
stored before it (including the published mirror's) still re-verify.

An artifact can carry a rendition too, and there the picture is
the measurement: rule draws the committed reference in one
lane, the artifact's own reconstruction in a second, and the absolute error
amplified by a fixed gain in a third. A reconstruction inside a tight bound
shows an almost blank error lane; a violation glows. The input is the committed
reference plus the reconstruction the artifact already carries, so a reader
re-derives the plot from data the claim covers — the bound stops being a number
in a JSON field and becomes something checkable by eye and by digest.
Bounded: lossy reconstructions with a promise

Real model classes — quantized weights, compressed embeddings, neural codec
outputs — cannot be hash-compared across hardware. The path
replaces equality with a metric promise: the challenge spec commits a
reference vector (bound by its specHash, exactly like a logic spec commits a
truth table), a metric ( — L∞, or — L1), and a
bound. The artifact carries the reconstruction. Verification is a pure
metric computation checked against the bound; the attestation records the
achieved , and the score is graded (perfect reconstruction 100,
at-bound 1, over-bound 0).

A length mismatch is an error, never a silent pass. Nobody can inflate a
metric's meaning after the fact: the metric, the reference, and the bound are
all committed before the artifact is verified.
